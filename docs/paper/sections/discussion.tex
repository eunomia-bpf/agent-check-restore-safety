\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What is agent-specific?}
The theory is intentionally reusable for adaptive runtimes.
Agents make the problem qualitatively prominent for four reasons.
They synthesize effect identities and arguments after restore rather than replaying a fixed instruction stream; they dynamically create branch and delegation structure; they mix local context with remote tools and human approvals; and they merge semantic artifacts whose provenance may span mutually exclusive searches.
The security definition does not depend on model nondeterminism, but the runtime phenomena are no longer exceptional failure paths.

\paragraph{Conditional guarantees are optional.}
A flat ledger that serializes actual consumption can enforce bounded use without knowing branch topology.
Our stronger contract matters when the runtime promises in advance that several alternatives can commit if retained.
Pure candidates can always use delayed escrow.
The paper does not claim topology-aware reservation strictly dominates it.
Instead, it states exactly when the stronger promise is solvent and how early escape changes that promise.

\paragraph{Trusted lifecycle evidence.}
The controller's \(\Phi\) is security-critical.
A system that cannot durably fence old continuations or attest selection cannot safely label a restore as replacing or branches as exclusive.
It may fall back to parallel compatibility, delayed escrow, or refusal.
This conservatism is a real deployment cost and should appear as false rejection or lost availability in evaluation.

\paragraph{Additive authority.}
Typed natural-number vectors cover one-shot approvals, quotas, budgets, and independently substitutable bounded effects.
They do not directly express temporal protocols, noncommutative obligations, disjunctive bundles, replenishing leases, or compensable authority.
For bundled issuance, the componentwise least repair may be unrealizable or have incomparable minimal bundles.
A richer authority algebra is future work; the concrete staging, support, and irreversibility distinctions should remain applicable.

\paragraph{External atomicity.}
The monitor accounts before dispatch.
Without sink-side stable tokens, it may have to charge an operation whose outcome is unknown, preserving safety at the expense of liveness.
It does not provide exactly-once effects at a Byzantine service.
Compensation is not history erasure and requires an explicitly authorized compensating effect.

\paragraph{Confidentiality and obligations.}
Issue-time disclosures are modeled as durable demand, but the current theorem is an integrity/solvency result.
It does not quantify information leakage or track obligations incurred by using authority.
Ghost-call privacy contracts and information-flow properties are complementary.

\paragraph{Ethics.}
The deterministic experiments use simulated payments, approvals, and services.
No real third-party resource should be modified and no production credential should be replayed.
Framework reports are used to establish lifecycle behavior, not to exploit deployed users.

\section{Conclusion}

Checkpoint safety is not achieved by rolling back more bytes.
History-transforming runtimes must preserve the distinction between branch-conditional commitments and effects that have become part of every future's durable past.
Authority continuity makes that distinction explicit.
It permits useful exclusive speculation, proves why replacing and live restore cannot be authorized from identical snapshot bytes alone, exposes merge as an authorization event when it retains alternative commitments, and explains why a losing branch's escaped effect cannot be repaired by abort.
For escape, the maximal-support construction retains exactly the futures that remain solvent; witnessed branch selection is a simpler conservative implementation.
As agent execution grows richer, this lifecycle contract is a more stable foundation than treating every restored call as either a replay or a fresh branch.
